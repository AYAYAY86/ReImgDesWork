The cursor is more than just a pointer; it shapes how users and graphic de-signers interact with digital tools. It acts as a kind of prosthesis, the mouse translates physical movement into digital action through x and y coordinates, enabling orientation and navigation in virtual space.   It becomes a visible ex-tension of the hand, linking gesture, thought, and screen. This creates a tactile dialogue between user and interface, where movement and representation are closely intertwined.

On trackpads and touchscreens, gestures like swiping and zooming mimic physical actions, making the interface feel more immediate and responsive.   The cursor is less visible in these contexts but still plays a key role, particularly in graphic design workflows where pointing, selecting, and manipulating ele-ments remain central tasks. As interaction methods evolve, the cursor contin-ues to mediate how we navigate and shape digital environments.

The cursor can be seen not only as a tool for performing actions, but also as a medium for generating artefacts through tracing, marking, and visualizing time, gesture, and motion. As one quote puts it, “Everyday objects and experi-ences with technology leave their mark on many areas of artistic production, which is now able to respond to current discourses. Examples include Marcel Duchamp’s readymades or the painting Nu Descendant un Escalier II (1912), which became relevant to film due to its multiple exposure effect.”   Similarly, the cursor’s path across the screen captures motion as a layered visual trace. For example, in my p5.js experiments, disabling the background() function lets each movement accumulate on the canvas — not only as a single mark, but as an echo of time, thought, and gesture. It's a visual parallel to Duchamp’s motion studies.
